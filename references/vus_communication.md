# Communicating a Variant of Uncertain Significance

## The problem

Families hear "variant of uncertain significance" and understand **"they found something
and they think it's probably bad but won't say so."**

That misreading is the default, not the exception. It causes months of avoidable
distress, and it sometimes causes real medical over-reaction — surveillance that isn't
indicated, family testing that answers nothing, treatment decisions built on a finding
that carries no information.

Getting this explanation right is one of the highest-value things this skill does, and
it is mostly a matter of saying two things clearly: **why uncertainty is normal**, and
**what would resolve it.**

## What a VUS actually is

A VUS means the laboratory looked at the evidence and it did not reach the threshold in
either direction. Not "leaning pathogenic." Not "pathogenic but cautious." **Genuinely
undetermined.**

Most VUS are eventually reclassified as benign. The base rate matters and families are
rarely told it.

The three reasons a variant sits in this category, in plain terms:

1. **It's rare.** Not enough other people with this exact change have been described.
2. **The gene is understudied.** Less is known about which changes in it matter.
3. **Ancestry gaps in reference databases.** Variants common in under-represented
   populations are more likely to be called uncertain simply because the databases are
   less complete. This is a known equity problem, and worth naming if relevant — it
   reframes the finding from "something wrong with my child" to "a gap in the reference
   data."

## Rules for handling a VUS in output

1. **Do not let a VUS drive surveillance.** Say explicitly that it should not.
2. **Do not let a VUS drive family testing** outside a research or genetics-directed
   context. Testing relatives for a VUS usually produces more uncertainty, not less.
3. **Do not apply the risk layer to a VUS.** A VUS carries no risk information. Do not
   describe the gene's associated syndrome as though the person has it.
4. **Do not rank VUS by "how suspicious" they look.** If asked which is most concerning,
   explain that this is exactly the judgement the lab declined to make, and route it to
   the genetics team.
5. **Do say what would resolve it** — this is the part that converts fear into a plan.

## What actually resolves a VUS

Give these concretely. They are real, they are things the family can ask about, and
knowing them is the difference between waiting helplessly and having a next step:

- **Parental testing (segregation).** Whether the variant arose new in the child or was
  inherited from an unaffected parent shifts the evidence substantially.
- **Time.** Databases grow. Variants get reclassified as more people are described.
  Reanalysis in two to three years is reasonable and is a thing to actively ask for.
- **RNA testing**, where the variant might affect splicing. Blood is often sufficient —
  a large majority of genes on intellectual disability and epilepsy panels are expressed
  in blood cells (*npj Genomic Medicine*, 2025). This is a real option families are
  rarely offered.
- **Functional studies**, for some genes, usually through research programmes.
- **Matchmaking** — connecting with other families who have variants in the same gene,
  via registries like Simons Searchlight or GeneMatcher.

## Language that works

**Instead of:** "A variant of uncertain significance was identified in GENE."
**Write:** "The lab found a change in GENE, but there isn't yet enough evidence to say
whether it's related to your child's development or is simply part of normal human
variation. Most changes in this category turn out to be harmless."

**Instead of:** "This VUS may be pathogenic."
**Write:** "Right now this finding doesn't tell us anything actionable. That's genuinely
what 'uncertain' means here — not a hidden 'probably bad.'"

**Instead of:** "Further evaluation is recommended."
**Write:** "Two things would help clarify this: testing both parents to see whether the
change is new in your child, and asking for the result to be reviewed again in two to
three years as more information becomes available."

## When the report has a VUS and a diagnostic finding

Lead with the diagnostic finding. Address the VUS separately and briefly, and say plainly
that it does not change the picture. Families otherwise conflate the two and end up
uncertain about a result that is not uncertain at all.

## When the report has only VUS

This is a hard conversation and worth handling with care. The honest content:

- The testing did not find an explanation. That is a real outcome, not a failure — and
  not the family's failure or the child's.
- It does not mean there is no genetic explanation; it means this test did not find one.
- Roughly 80–90% of autism/NDD testing is non-diagnostic. They are in the majority.
- There are concrete next steps: parental testing, reanalysis on a defined timeline,
  possibly broader testing if only a panel or microarray was done.
- **The child's care does not depend on this result.** Support, therapy, and services are
  driven by needs, not by genetic findings. This is often the most important sentence in
  the whole output, because families sometimes believe a diagnosis is the gate to
  services. Usually it is not.
